        Bench tile: full build guide

        A plywood plate on four springs pushes a rack. The rack turns a pinion, a belt makes a stepper motor spin almost four times faster, and the motor works as a generator. Two diode bridges turn its AC into DC, which heats a known resistor; an Arduino measures the voltage across that resistor and adds up each step's energy. Allow two afternoons: one for the frame, one for the board.

        1 · How the circuit works

        GENERATORStepper motorTwo separate coils around a magnet rotor. Spin the shaft and each coil makes its own AC; faster spin, more voltage.
        RECTIFIERSTwo diode bridgesOne per coil, four diodes each. They flip the negative half-waves positive, so both coils feed one DC pair without fighting.
        WHY SCHOTTKY1N5819 diodesAbout 0.3 V lost per diode instead of 0.7 V. Two conduct at a time, and a gentle step makes only a few volts.
        LOADR1, 10 ΩAll the step's electricity turns into heat here. Power is V² ÷ R, so voltage alone tells you the energy.
        MEASUREMENTDivider, Zener, filterR2 and R3 divide the voltage by 5.7 so up to 28 V reads as 0–5 V. The 5.1 V Zener clamps a hard stomp; C1 removes noise.

        4 · Tools and safety

        • Temperature-controlled soldering iron, chisel or conical tip
        • Lead-free rosin-core solder, 0.8 mm, and a flux pen
        • Brass-wool tip cleaner, helping hands or a small vise
        • Flush cutters, wire strippers, needle-nose pliers
        • Multimeter with resistance, continuity and diode test
        • Desoldering braid, heat-shrink and a heat gun or lighter
        • Calipers (or a ruler and feeler shims) for setting travel
        • Safety glasses on: trimmed leads and solder spatter fly.
        • A fan pulling flux smoke away from your face; window open.
        • The iron's metal is at 340 °C: always return it to its stand.
        • Wash your hands after soldering, before eating.
        • Never connect the + rail straight to the Arduino: only A0 through the divider.
        • If you're new to soldering, practise ten joints on a spare piece of perfboard first.

        5 · Build the frame

        1. Cut two 60 × 60 cm plywood squares. Drill a 10 mm hole 3 cm in from each corner of the base; glue 8 mm dowels into matching spots on the top plate so they slide in the holes as plungers.
        2. Slip a spring over each dowel between the plates. Unloaded, the top plate should sit about 10 mm above the end stops.
        3. Screw the rack upright to the underside of the top plate, near one edge, teeth facing inward.
        4. Screw two KFL08 bearings to a wood block on the base. Push the 8 mm shaft through them with the pinion meshing on the rack and the 60-tooth pulley on the other end. Tighten both set screws on the shaft's flat.
        5. Mount the stepper on a second block with the 16-tooth pulley on its 5 mm shaft. Fit the belt, slide the motor back until the belt is snug, and screw it down. Without this 3.75× step-up the motor turns too slowly to get past the diodes.
        6. Glue four end-stop blocks to the base under the corners. Stack washers on them until the plate stops after 6 mm of travel; you'll change this to 4 and 8 mm in testing.
        7. There is no one-way clutch on the bench tile. On the way back up the springs turn the motor backwards; the bridges rectify that too, so you also measure the energy the springs give back.

        8 · First power-up

        1. Find the coil pairs with the ohmmeter. Put one pair in J1's A+ and A−, the other in B+ and B−. Which wire of a pair goes where doesn't matter; the bridge rectifies either way.
        2. Meter on DC volts across R1. Spin the big pulley by hand: the reading should flick up to a few volts.
        3. Move the meter to A0 and GND. Stomp on the plate as hard as you can: it must stay under 5 V.
        4. Plug the Nano into a laptop by USB, connect the A0 and GND leads, upload the sketch below, and open Serial Monitor at 115200 baud.
        5. Step on the tile. Each step prints one line: step number, joules, peak volts and the running average.

        9 · Logger sketch and calibration

        Before trusting the numbers, measure three things and type them into the top of the sketch: R1 with its leads (R_LOAD), R2 and R3 to work out (R2 + R3) ÷ R3 (DIVIDER), and the Nano's 5V pin while on USB (VREF; often 4.7–5.1 V). The output is comma-separated, so you can paste it straight into a spreadsheet.

        // Harvest Steps bench logger v2: energy of each step into the load resistor
// Put your own measurements in the four values below.
const float R_LOAD  = 10.0;   // ohms: R1 plus its leads, measured
const float DIVIDER = 5.70;   // (R2 + R3) / R3, from measured values
const float VREF    = 5.00;   // volts on the Nano's 5V pin, measured on USB
const float THRESH  = 0.20;   // volts across R1 that count as "producing"
const unsigned long DT_US    = 500;  // sample every 0.5 ms (2 kHz)
const unsigned long QUIET_MS = 400;  // this long below THRESH ends a step

float joules = 0, peakV = 0, total = 0;
unsigned long quietSince = 0;
int steps = 0;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  Serial.println("step,joules,peak_volts,average_joules");
}

void loop() {
  static unsigned long next = micros();
  while ((long)(micros() - next) < 0) {}    // wait for the next sample
  next += DT_US;

  float v = analogRead(A0) * VREF / 1023.0 * DIVIDER;   // volts across R1
  if (v > THRESH) {
    joules += v * v / R_LOAD * DT_US * 1e-6;              // P = V^2/R, times dt
    if (v > peakV) peakV = v;
    quietSince = millis();
  } else if (joules > 0 && millis() - quietSince > QUIET_MS) {
    steps++;
    total += joules;
    Serial.print(steps);      Serial.print(',');
    Serial.print(joules, 4);  Serial.print(',');
    Serial.print(peakV, 2);   Serial.print(',');
    Serial.println(total / steps, 4);
    joules = 0;  peakV = 0;
  }
}

        10 · Test plan

        1. Weigh three walkers of different builds on a bathroom scale.
        2. With the end stops at 4 mm, each walker takes 20 normal steps across the tile. Save the serial output.
        3. Repeat at 6 mm and 8 mm. Average each walker's joules at each travel.
        4. In the Energy physics tab, set the mass and travel, then adjust the efficiencies until the prediction matches your average. That tells you which stage loses the most.
        5. If 6 mm gives under 1 J, raise the step-up ratio (a 20T pinion or a bigger pulley) before changing anything else.
