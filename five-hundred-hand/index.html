The Five Hundred Hand Project

A compact Bluetooth game controller with one drift-free stick, designed from the start to be made in a batch of 30, for about $20 of parts each.

Where this stands: a design, not yet a built controller. No circuit board has been ordered and nothing here has been tested on hardware. The firmware compiles without errors; it has never run on a real board. Prices were looked up in November 2026 and some are marked as unconfirmed. The plan below starts with a breadboard and two prototype boards for exactly that reason: find the mistakes on $40 of parts, not on 30 boards.

What it is

  • Inputs: one magnetic stick with a click (L3), four face buttons, L1, R1, L2 and R2, plus Select, Start and Home. No d-pad and no right stick.
  • Connection: Bluetooth Low Energy, as a standard HID gamepad. Windows and macOS pair with it from their normal Bluetooth settings; nothing to install.
  • Power: a 1,200 mAh lithium-polymer cell charged through USB-C, a slide switch, and an estimated 25 to 40 hours of play per charge (worked out from typical current figures for this chip, not measured).
  • Size: about 134 × 80 × 24 mm and roughly 110 g. That is Joy-Con-pair width with short grips; it is thicker than a Switch Lite because a full-size stick is 19.5 mm tall.

The five design decisions

1. The chip: ESP32-C6-WROOM-1

The controller is built around Espressif's ESP32-C6-WROOM-1 module. Four reasons:

  • It is a finished, shielded radio with its own antenna and its own FCC ID. You never design radio circuitry, and it matters a great deal if you ever sell a unit (see below).
  • It has USB built in. Each board is flashed through its own USB-C socket. No programmer, no extra chip, which is what makes 30 units practical.
  • It has enough pins. Twelve buttons, two stick axes, battery sensing and a light need 16 pins. The ESP32-C3 module has 13 free, which would mean adding a second chip to read buttons, and it costs the same. The C6 has more than 20, so every button gets its own pin and the wiring stays simple.
  • It is easy to program with the free Arduino software, and at $3.48 it is the cheapest module that does all of the above.

The smaller MINI version of the same chip would save space, but JLCPCB only fits it on its dearer "Standard" assembly service. The WROOM version is accepted on the cheap "Economic" one.

What you give up: a Nordic nRF52840 uses roughly a tenth of the power and would run for weeks, not days. It costs about $2 more per unit and needs a separate programmer for every board. Battery life is last on this project's priority list and 25-plus hours is already more than most store-bought pads, so the ESP32-C6 wins. If you want month-long battery life later, the nRF52840 is the upgrade.

2. Shoulder inputs: four digital buttons, no analog triggers

Use two buttons a side, L1 / R1 and L2 / R2, all simple on/off switches. The firmware reports L2 and R2 both as buttons and as fully pressed triggers, so games that expect a trigger still respond.

  • Analog triggers matter in two kinds of game: racing games (how hard you press the throttle) and shooters. Shooters need a right stick, which this controller does not have. That leaves racing, where an on/off trigger still works; you just cannot feather the throttle.
  • An analog trigger is the hardest mechanical part of a controller: a pivoting lever, a return spring, a magnet and a sensor, twice. Printed in plastic by a first-time builder, it is the part most likely to break or stick in a backpack. Durability is this project's first priority.
  • Four digital buttons cost about 40 cents and are placed by the board factory.

The two trigger pins were chosen from the ones that can also read a voltage, so a later board could swap the L2 and R2 switches for magnetic sensors without moving anything else.

3. The stick: a full-size magnetic module

Use a full-size stick that reads its position with magnets and no rubbing contacts, the same shape as the one in PlayStation and Xbox controllers: either a Hall-effect module or its newer cousin, TMR. Both sense a magnet without touching it, which is why they do not develop drift as they wear. The one this design is drawn around is the YTL 13-series, sold in a Hall version and a TMR version. The TMR version's datasheet confirms it runs on 3.3 V, draws about 0.2 mA, has a built-in click switch and is rated for 5 million movements; the Hall version is cheaper and its datasheet still needs reading before you order.

The small Joy-Con style sticks would make the controller thinner, but they connect with a tiny ribbon and socket, screw to the case and not the board, and have no published datasheet. The full-size module is 14 ordinary solder joints and sits on the board by its own metal legs, which is far more forgiving for a first build and far stronger in a bag.

4. Face buttons: a silicone pad with carbon contacts

Store-bought controllers get their soft, quiet press from a moulded silicone dome with a carbon pill under it; pressing the button squashes the dome and the pill bridges two bare gold contacts on the board. This design does the same. The board has four gold contact patterns, and a replacement rubber pad made for a Super Nintendo style controller sits on top with a printed cap on each dome.

That pad was chosen because it is cheap, has been made unchanged for decades by many factories, and open-hardware board designs exist that show its contact layout. Select, Start and Home are small soft-press switches placed by the factory; the four shoulder buttons are side-facing switches, which do click lightly, as the bumpers on most commercial controllers do.

5. Power: charging that works whether the switch is on or off

A USB-C socket feeds a charger chip and the battery. A protection chip on the board cuts the battery off if it is over-charged, run flat or shorted, in addition to the one inside the cell. The slide switch does not carry the battery's current: it only tells the voltage regulator to turn on or off, so the switch's tiny contacts are never stressed and the controller draws almost nothing when off. A small transistor also switches the stick's power off when the controller sleeps.

The rest of the circuit, connection by connection

  • USB-C socket: VBUS to the charger's input and to D1. CC1 and CC2 each through their own 5.1 kΩ resistor to ground (without these, a USB-C to USB-C cable supplies no power). D− and D+ to GPIO12 and GPIO13. Shell and GND pins to ground. 10 µF from VBUS to ground.
  • TP4056 charger: VCC and CE to VBUS. PROG through 3 kΩ to ground (sets 400 mA, a gentle third of the cell's capacity). BAT to battery +, with 10 µF to ground. CHRG and STDBY each pull an LED (red = charging, green = full) through 1 kΩ from VBUS. TEMP to ground.
  • Battery protection (XB5353A): wired exactly as the "typical application" drawing in its datasheet, between the cell's minus lead and circuit ground, with the one resistor and one capacitor that drawing shows.
  • Q1 (AO3401A) and D1 (Schottky): D1 from VBUS to the system rail. Q1's drain to battery +, source to the system rail, gate to VBUS with 100 kΩ from gate to ground. Cable in: the gate is high, Q1 is off, the rail runs from USB. Cable out: the gate falls, Q1 turns on.
  • Regulator (ME6211C33): input from the system rail with 10 µF to ground; output is the 3.3 V rail with 10 µF to ground. Its enable pin goes to the middle pin of the slide switch; the switch's two outer pins go to the system rail (on) and ground (off).
  • Q2 (AO3401A): source to 3.3 V, gate to GPIO8, drain to the stick's two supply pins, with 100 nF from the drain to ground.
  • Stick: each axis has three pins: supply, output, ground. The pin order is reversed between the green-bodied and blue-bodied versions of the module. Check the datasheet for the colour you bought before drawing the footprint.

What it does

  • Reads every input about a thousand times a second. A press is reported on its very first contact, then that button is ignored for 5 ms while the contact settles, so cleaning up the signal adds no delay.
  • Sends a report only when something changed, and at most once per radio slot, so reports never queue up.
  • Asks the computer for the shortest gap between radio slots: 7.5 ms, which Windows allows; if that is refused, 11.25 ms, then 15 ms, which are what Apple's rules allow.
  • Remembers the stick's centre and range. Hold Start + Select for 3 seconds to calibrate: leave the stick alone while the light is solid, then roll it around its edge while the light flickers.
  • Reports the battery level to the computer and double-blinks the light under 10 %.
  • Sleeps after 10 minutes untouched or 3 minutes with no computer found. Home or Start wakes it. Hold Home for 5 seconds to forget every paired computer.

Setting up and flashing

  1. Install the Arduino IDE 2.
  2. In Boards Manager, install "esp32" by Espressif Systems (version 3). In Library Manager, install "NimBLE-Arduino" by h2zero (version 2).
  3. Put five_hundred_hand.ino in a folder with the same name, five_hundred_hand, and open it.
  4. Under Tools: Board "ESP32C6 Dev Module"; USB CDC On Boot "Enabled"; CPU Frequency "80MHz (WiFi)"; Flash Size "8MB (64Mb)".
  5. Plug the board in with a USB-C cable that carries data (many charge-only cables do not), pick its port under Tools > Port, and press Upload.
  6. If the port does not appear or the upload fails: hold the BOOT button, switch the controller off and on, release BOOT, and upload again.
  7. Open Tools > Serial Monitor. Twice a second the controller prints which buttons are down, the stick position and the battery level. Use this to check a board before it goes in a case.
  8. On the computer, open Bluetooth settings and add "Five Hundred Hand". On Windows, joy.cpl (Set up USB game controllers) shows every button and axis.

For the batch, flash the first board from the Arduino IDE, then use Sketch > Export Compiled Binary and Espressif's free esptool to write that one file to the other 29. It takes about 20 seconds a board.

What "compiles" does and does not mean. The sketch was built for the ESP32-C6 with version 3.3.12 of the esp32 board package and version 2.5.2 of NimBLE-Arduino, with no errors or warnings. That catches typing mistakes, not behaviour. Pairing, the report layout, sleep and calibration all still need checking on a real board, which is step one of what to do first.

The circuit board

Software

Use EasyEDA (free, runs in the browser). It is made by the same company as JLCPCB and the LCSC parts shop, so every part in the list below can be dropped in by its "C" number with a correct footprint, and the order files for assembly come out with one click. KiCad is the stronger tool and worth learning later, but for a first board that a factory will assemble, EasyEDA removes the two most common beginner mistakes: wrong footprints and wrong part rotations.

The board

  • Size and build: about 122 × 44 mm, two copper layers, 1.2 mm thick (the stick's legs are made for a 1.2 mm board), with ENIG gold finish. Gold is not optional: the face buttons press carbon onto bare board contacts, and the ordinary tin finish wears and tarnishes there.
  • Everything the factory places goes on the front. Assembly on one side is the cheap service; two sides roughly doubles the set-up cost.
  • Radio module at the top edge, in the middle, with its antenna end overhanging the edge, or with no copper on either layer under the antenna, and nothing metal within about 10 mm of it. The USB-C socket goes beside it on the same edge.
  • Stick on the left, face contacts on the right. Draw the stick's holes from its datasheet: four of 1.2 mm for the frame legs, six of 1.0 mm for the sensor pins, four of 0.9 mm for the switch.
  • Face contacts: for each button, two interleaved combs of 0.3 mm fingers with 0.3 mm gaps, in a circle 1 mm wider than the pad's carbon pill, with the green solder mask removed over the whole circle. One comb goes to the pin, the other to ground. Their spacing comes from measuring the pad you bought, along with the holes for its locating pegs.
  • Shoulder switches at the top corners, facing outward over the edge. Small soft switches for Select, Start and Home where the drawing shows them, and the BOOT switch anywhere a pin through a hole in the back can reach.
  • Tracks: 0.5 mm or wider for anything carrying battery or USB power, 0.25 mm for signals, the back layer one solid ground area. Keep the USB data pair short and side by side.
  • Mounting: four 2.2 mm holes for M2 screws, plus a notch in the bottom edge for the battery lead to pass to the back.
  • Test pads: 3.3 V, ground, battery +, TX, RX, labelled in the white print.

Ordering from JLCPCB

  1. In EasyEDA, run the design check until it reports no errors, then choose "Order at JLCPCB". (From KiCad: export Gerber files, a parts list and a placement file.)
  2. Choose 2 layers, 1.2 mm thickness, ENIG finish. Any colour.
  3. Turn on PCB Assembly: "Economic", top side. For the first order choose 5 boards with 2 assembled; for the batch, assemble all.
  4. Check the parts list it matched. "Basic" parts carry no extra fee; each different "Extended" part adds a small one-off fee to the order. If a part is out of stock, pick the suggested equivalent and check its footprint is the same.
  5. Look at the placement preview carefully. Check the dot or notch on every chip, the direction of the diode and LEDs, and that the USB-C socket faces off the board. Wrong rotation is the classic first-order mistake.
  6. Pay. Boards usually arrive in one to two weeks. The price at checkout includes any import duty for your country: use that number, not an estimate.

How the parts mount

  • Board: sits on four posts in the front shell, located by its four holes, and is clamped by matching posts on the back shell.
  • Stick: soldered to the board; the cap pushes onto its 4 mm D-shaped shaft after the case is closed.
  • Face buttons: caps drop into their holes from inside the front shell, the silicone pad lies over them with its pegs through the board, and the board holds the stack in place.
  • Shoulder buttons: each is a printed plunger sliding in a slot in the top edge, with a flange inside so it cannot pull out, pressing the side-facing switch. No hinge and no spring: the switch returns it.
  • Battery: in a walled pocket in the back shell, held with thin foam tape. No screw posts, sharp edges or wire ends anywhere near it.
  • Light: a 2 mm hole over the status LED, filled with a drop of clear glue as a window.

Printing

  • PETG, not PLA. PLA softens at about 55 °C, which a car in summer reaches. PETG is tougher and bends before it snaps.
  • 0.2 mm layers, 4 walls, 25 % infill, front shell printed face-down on a textured plate so the face comes out even.
  • Print one small test piece first with a button hole, a screw post and the USB opening, and adjust the clearances for your printer before printing 30 cases.
  • Each case is roughly 3 to 4 hours and 65 to 75 g of filament.

Assembly, step by step

The factory has already soldered every small part. Per controller, you solder one part (the stick) and assemble.

  1. Look the board over. Check the USB-C socket and the module are flat and straight.
  2. Flash and test the bare board before soldering anything. Plug it in, upload the firmware, open the Serial Monitor, and press each switch with a fingertip; bridge each gold face contact with the tip of a metal screwdriver. Every one should show up.
  3. Solder the stick. Push it flat against the board, solder one frame leg, check it is still flat, then solder the other 13 joints. Iron at about 350 °C, 2 seconds a joint.
  4. Check the battery plug with a multimeter before it ever touches the board. Red must line up with the pad marked +. Sellers do not agree on which way round this plug is wired, and a reversed battery destroys the board. If it is reversed, lift the two metal tabs out of the plug housing and swap them.
  5. Plug in the battery, switch on. The light should blink slowly. Pair it with a computer and check every input in joy.cpl or a browser gamepad tester.
  6. Calibrate the stick: hold Start + Select for 3 seconds and follow the light.
  7. Charge test: plug in USB with the switch off. Red light on; it turns green when full.
  8. Set the six brass inserts into the front shell's posts with the soldering iron at about 220 °C. Press gently and let the heat do the work.
  9. Load the front shell, lying face-down: four button caps, the silicone pad, the four shoulder plungers, the small button caps, then lower the board onto its posts.
  10. Fit the battery into the back shell with foam tape, route its lead through the board's notch, and close the case. Six screws, snug, not tight.
  11. Push on the thumb cap and run the full test once more.

Time: the first one will take over an hour. Once practised, expect about 20 to 25 minutes of hands-on work per controller, so roughly 12 hours for 30, plus about 100 hours of unattended printing.

Tools you need: a temperature-controlled soldering iron (about $25 to $40), thin solder, a multimeter ($15), flush cutters, tweezers, a small screwdriver and digital calipers ($15). Practise on a $5 solder practice kit first; the stick is not the part to learn on.

If you sell them

This is the part most hobby write-ups leave out, and it changes the answer. This is general information, not legal advice.

  • FCC. Because the radio is a pre-certified module used as its maker intended, the controller does not need its own radio certification. It still needs testing as an ordinary electronic device (FCC Part 15, Subpart B) and a Supplier's Declaration of Conformity. Labs quote roughly $1,500 to $2,500 for that; some quote up to $5,000. The case must be labelled "Contains FCC ID:" followed by the module's ID, and a person or company in the United States must be named as responsible. Without a certified module, radio certification runs about $8,000 to $25,000, which is the best argument for using one.
  • Bluetooth. The Bluetooth SIG requires every product that uses Bluetooth to be qualified under the seller's own membership before sale. Joining as an Adopter is free. The qualification fee, on the schedule in force since 1 March 2026, is $12,000 per design. Using a module that is already qualified removes the testing, not the fee, and no reduced fee for small makers could be found.
  • So the cost of selling legally is about $13,500 to $14,500 before the first sale: around $450 a unit at 30 units, or $70 a unit at 200. That is why small makers generally do not sell Bluetooth devices, and it is the honest answer for this project too.
  • Thirty units for friends is not automatically exempt. The FCC's exemption for home-built devices covers things that are not marketed and are built in quantities of five or fewer for personal use.
  • Also on the list: a lithium battery test summary (UN 38.3) from the cell's maker for shipping; California's embedded-battery recycling fee and labelling rules; and, if it is sold as a product for children 12 and under, third-party safety testing and a Children's Product Certificate.
  • Looks and names. Sony lists the PlayStation shapes (triangle, circle, cross, square) as its trademarks and has protected the shape of its controllers. On a unit you sell, use your own button markings and your own body shape, and do not describe it with another company's product names.

Honest trade-offs against a store-bought controller

  • It will not save money. A good budget Bluetooth controller with two sticks, a d-pad, rumble and motion sensing costs $20 to $30 in a shop. This one costs about $20 in parts plus your time, tools and prints. Build it to learn how a controller works and to own a design you can change.
  • "Standard gamepad" has limits on both systems. Windows lists it as a game controller with no driver, and Steam, browsers and emulators use it directly. But many Windows games only listen for Xbox-style controllers and will need Steam Input or a similar remapper in between. On a Mac it pairs and works in Steam, browsers and emulators, but games that use Apple's own controller system only recognise Xbox, PlayStation and a few other named pads. Some hobby firmware gets around this by pretending to be an Xbox controller; this one does not, because copying another company's product identity is not something to ship.
  • Latency is good, not the best. At 7.5 ms between radio slots a press waits about 4 ms on average, 7.5 ms at worst, before it is sent. That is fine for fast games. A commercial pad on its own USB dongle, or wired, is quicker, and this controller has no wired mode. On a Mac the gap is 11 to 15 ms.
  • Fewer inputs. No right stick, no d-pad, no analog triggers, no rumble, no motion. Great for platformers, retro and arcade games; wrong for shooters.
  • Feel. The face buttons use the same rubber-dome method as commercial pads, so the press itself should feel right. The printed shell and caps will not: layer lines, a slightly rough surface and looser fits than injection-moulded plastic.
  • Thicker. About 24 mm, against 14 mm for a Switch Lite, because of the full-size stick.
  • Durability is designed in but unproven. PETG shells, brass inserts, a supported USB socket and a contact-free stick are the right choices. A printed case is still weaker between its layers than a moulded one, and none of this has been through a term in a backpack yet.
  • No safety testing. A commercial controller's battery circuit has been tested and certified. This one uses standard, well-proven parts, but nobody has tested this board. Do not charge the first units unattended.

What to do first

  1. Breadboard it (about $25). Buy one ESP32-C6-DevKitC-1 development board (it carries the same module), two sticks, one rubber pad set and a handful of push buttons. Wire them to the pins in the table, flash the firmware and get it working as a gamepad on your computer. This proves the firmware and tells you how the stick and pad feel before any money goes on boards.
  2. Measure. With calipers, measure the pad (dome spacing, pill size, peg positions) and the stick. Those numbers go into the board and the case.
  3. Draw the board and order 5, with 2 assembled (about $45 to $60). Build two complete controllers with printed cases. Expect to find at least one mistake; that is what this order is for.
  4. Use those two for a few weeks, in a bag, every day. Fix what breaks.
  5. Then order the 30.
