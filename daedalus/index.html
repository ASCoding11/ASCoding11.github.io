Project Daedalus

A compact Bluetooth game controller with one drift-free stick, designed from the start to be made in a batch of 30, for $23 to $28 of parts each.

Where this stands: a design, not yet a built controller. No circuit board has been ordered and nothing here has been tested on hardware. The firmware compiles without errors; it has never run on a real board. Prices were looked up in November 2026 and some are marked as unconfirmed. The plan below starts with a breadboard and two prototype boards for exactly that reason: find the mistakes on $50 of parts, not on 30 boards.

What it is

  • Inputs: one magnetic stick with a click (L3), four face buttons, L1, R1, L2 and R2, plus Select, Start and Home. No d-pad and no right stick.
  • Connection: Bluetooth Low Energy, as a standard HID gamepad. Windows and macOS pair with it from their normal Bluetooth settings; nothing to install.
  • Power: a 1,200 mAh lithium-polymer cell charged through USB-C, a slide switch, and an estimated 30 hours of play per charge (worked out from the datasheets, not measured).
  • Size: 125 × 78 mm, 22.2 mm thick in the middle and 29.2 mm front to back at the grips, and roughly 100 g: narrower than a pair of Joy-Cons side by side, with short rounded grips that fill the palm. It is thicker than a Switch Lite because a full-size stick is 19.5 mm tall.

      The five design decisions

      1. The chip: ESP32-C6-WROOM-1

      The controller is built around Espressif's ESP32-C6-WROOM-1 module. Four reasons:

      • It is a finished, shielded radio with its own antenna and its own FCC ID. You never design radio circuitry, and it matters a great deal if you ever sell a unit (see below).
      • It has USB built in. Each board is flashed through its own USB-C socket. No programmer, no extra chip, which is what makes 30 units practical.
      • It has enough pins. Twelve buttons, two stick axes, battery sensing and a light need 16 pins. The ESP32-C3 module has 13 free, which would mean adding a second chip to read buttons, and it costs the same. The C6 has more than 20, so every button gets its own pin and the wiring stays simple.
      • It is easy to program with the free Arduino software, and at $3.49 it is among the cheapest modules that do all of the above.

      The smaller MINI version of the same chip would save space, but JLCPCB only fits it on its dearer "Standard" assembly service. The WROOM version is accepted on the cheap "Economic" one.

      What you give up: a Nordic nRF52840 uses roughly a tenth of the power and would run for weeks, not days. It costs about $2 more per unit and needs a separate programmer for every board. Battery life is last on this project's priority list and 25-plus hours is already more than most store-bought pads, so the ESP32-C6 wins. If you want month-long battery life later, the nRF52840 is the upgrade.

      2. Shoulder inputs: four digital buttons, no analog triggers

      Use two buttons a side, L1 / R1 and L2 / R2, all simple on/off switches. The firmware reports L2 and R2 both as buttons and as fully pressed triggers, so games that expect a trigger still respond.

      • Analog triggers matter in two kinds of game: racing games (how hard you press the throttle) and shooters. Shooters need a right stick, which this controller does not have. That leaves racing, where an on/off trigger still works; you just cannot feather the throttle.
      • An analog trigger is the hardest mechanical part of a controller: a pivoting lever, a return spring, a magnet and a sensor, twice. Printed in plastic by a first-time builder, it is the part most likely to break or stick in a backpack. Durability is this project's first priority.
      • Four digital buttons cost about 40 cents and are placed by the board factory.

      The two trigger pins were chosen from the ones that can also read a voltage, so a later board could swap the L2 and R2 switches for magnetic sensors without moving anything else.

      3. The stick: a full-size magnetic module

      Use a full-size stick that reads its position with magnets and no rubbing contacts, the same shape as the one in PlayStation and Xbox controllers: either a Hall-effect module or its newer cousin, TMR. Both sense a magnet without touching it, which is why they do not develop drift as they wear. The one this design is drawn around is the YTL 13-series, sold in a Hall version and a TMR version. Both datasheets confirm the stick runs on 3.3 V and has a built-in click switch rated for a million presses. The Hall version draws about 2 mA and is the cheaper one; the TMR version draws a tenth of that. Either is a small load next to the radio.

      The small Joy-Con style sticks would make the controller thinner, but they connect with a tiny ribbon and socket, screw to the case and not the board, and have no published datasheet. The full-size module is 14 ordinary solder joints and sits on the board by its own metal legs, which is far more forgiving for a first build and far stronger in a bag.

      4. Face buttons: a silicone pad with carbon contacts

      Store-bought controllers get their soft, quiet press from a moulded silicone dome with a carbon pill under it; pressing the button squashes the dome and the pill bridges two bare gold contacts on the board. This design does the same. The board has four gold contact patterns, and a replacement rubber pad made for a Super Nintendo style controller sits on top with a printed cap on each dome.

      That pad was chosen because it has been made unchanged for decades by many factories, and an open-hardware board design exists that shows its contact layout: the four contacts sit 27 mm apart left to right and 21 mm top to bottom. Select, Start and Home are small soft-press switches placed by the factory; the four shoulder buttons are side-facing switches, which do click lightly, as the bumpers on most commercial controllers do.

      5. Power: charging that works whether the switch is on or off

      A USB-C socket feeds a charger chip and the battery. A protection chip on the board cuts the battery off if it is over-charged, run flat or shorted, in addition to the one inside the cell. The slide switch does not carry the battery's current: it only tells the voltage regulator to turn on or off, so the switch's tiny contacts are never stressed and the controller draws almost nothing when off. A small transistor also switches the stick's power off when the controller sleeps.

      The rest of the circuit, connection by connection

      • USB-C socket: VBUS to the charger's input and to D1. CC1 and CC2 each through their own 5.1 kΩ resistor (R1, R2) to ground (without these, a USB-C to USB-C cable supplies no power). D− and D+ to GPIO12 and GPIO13. Shell and GND pins to ground. 10 µF (C1) from VBUS to ground.
      • TP4056 charger: VCC and CE to VBUS. PROG through 3 kΩ (R3) to ground (sets about 0.37 A, a gentle third of the cell's capacity; the current is 1,100 divided by the resistance in ohms). BAT to battery +, with 10 µF (C2) to ground. CHRG and STDBY each pull an LED (red = charging, green = full) through 1 kΩ from VBUS: LED2 with R13 on CHRG, LED3 with R14 on STDBY. TEMP to ground.
      • Battery protection (XB5353A): the cell's minus lead goes to the chip's GND pin (pin 2) and nowhere else. Pins 4 and 5 (VM) are the circuit's ground. Pin 3 (VDD) connects to battery + through 100 Ω (R4), with 100 nF (C8) from pin 3 to pin 2. Pin 1 is left unconnected. It cuts off above 4.30 V, below 2.40 V and at about 3 A.
      • Q1 (AO3401A) and D1 (Schottky): D1 from VBUS to the system rail. Q1's drain to battery +, source to the system rail, gate to VBUS with 100 kΩ (R5) from gate to ground. Cable in: the gate is high, Q1 is off, the rail runs from USB. Cable out: the gate falls, Q1 turns on.
      • Regulator (ME6211C33): input from the system rail with 10 µF (C3) to ground; output is the 3.3 V rail with 10 µF (C4) to ground. Its enable pin goes to the middle pin of the slide switch; the switch's two outer pins go to the system rail (on) and ground (off).
      • Q2 (AO3401A): source to 3.3 V, gate to GPIO8, drain to the stick's two supply pins, with 100 nF (C9) from the drain to ground.
      • Stick: each of its two sensors has three pins. On the Hall module listed here both sensors have blue housings and the order is pin 1 ground, pin 2 output, pin 3 supply. Sensors with green housings are the other way round (pin 1 supply, pin 3 ground), and some versions of the TMR module have one of each, so look at the colours of the part you actually receive. The click switch's four pins are two pairs: S1 and S2 are joined, S3 and S4 are joined; connect one pair to GPIO4 and the other to ground.

      What it does

      • Reads every input about a thousand times a second. A press is reported on its very first contact, then that button is ignored for 5 ms while the contact settles, so cleaning up the signal adds no delay.
      • Sends a report only when something changed, and at most once per radio slot, so reports never queue up.
      • Asks the computer for the shortest gap between radio slots: 7.5 ms, which Windows allows; if that is refused, 11.25 ms, then 15 ms, which are what Apple's rules allow.
      • Remembers the stick's centre and range. Hold Start + Select for 3 seconds to calibrate: leave the stick alone while the light is solid, then roll it around its edge while the light flickers.
      • Gives the stick a round dead zone, measured on how far it is pushed and not on each axis separately, so slow diagonal moves do not snap to up, down, left or right.
      • Reports the battery level to the computer and double-blinks the light under 10 %.
      • Sleeps after 10 minutes untouched or 3 minutes with no computer found. Home or Start wakes it. Hold Home for 5 seconds to forget every paired computer.

      Setting up and flashing

      1. Install the Arduino IDE 2.
      2. In Boards Manager, install "esp32" by Espressif Systems (version 3). In Library Manager, install "NimBLE-Arduino" by h2zero (version 2).
      3. Put daedalus.ino in a folder with the same name, daedalus, and open it.
      4. Under Tools: Board "ESP32C6 Dev Module"; USB CDC On Boot "Enabled"; CPU Frequency "80MHz (WiFi)"; Flash Size "8MB (64Mb)".
      5. Plug the board in with a USB-C cable that carries data (many charge-only cables do not), pick its port under Tools > Port, and press Upload.
      6. If the port does not appear or the upload fails: hold the BOOT button, switch the controller off and on, release BOOT, and upload again. On a finished controller that means taking the back off first; it should only ever be needed if firmware has gone badly wrong.
      7. Open Tools > Serial Monitor. Twice a second the controller prints which buttons are down, the stick position and the battery level. Use this to check a board before it goes in a case.
      8. On the computer, open Bluetooth settings and add "Daedalus". On Windows, joy.cpl (Set up USB game controllers) shows every button and axis.

      If you prefer PlatformIO to the Arduino IDE, the folder also has a platformio.ini that pins the exact versions: put it beside the sketch and run pio run -t upload.

      On a breadboard first

      • The ESP32-C6-DevKitC-1's pins are labelled with the same GPIO numbers as the table above, so wire each push button from its pin to a ground rail.
      • Power the stick straight from the board's 3V3 pin (the transistor that switches it off is only on the finished board). Each axis needs 3V3, ground and its output to GPIO2 or GPIO3; the click switch goes between GPIO4 and ground.
      • For the status light, put any LED in series with a 1 kΩ resistor between GPIO15 and ground, long leg towards GPIO15.
      • Leave GPIO6 unconnected: with no battery the level will read as empty, which is fine for testing.
      • The development board has two USB-C sockets. Use the one marked USB, not UART.

      For the batch, flash the first board from the Arduino IDE, then use Sketch > Export Compiled Binary and Espressif's free esptool to write that one file to the other 29. It takes about 20 seconds a board.

      What "compiles" does and does not mean. The sketch was built for the ESP32-C6 with version 3.3.12 of the esp32 board package and version 2.5.2 of NimBLE-Arduino, with no errors or warnings. That catches typing mistakes, not behaviour. Pairing, the report layout, sleep and calibration all still need checking on a real board, which is step one of what to do first.

      Software

      Use EasyEDA (free, runs in the browser). It is made by the same company as JLCPCB and the LCSC parts shop, so every part in the list below can be dropped in by its "C" number with a correct footprint, and the order files for assembly come out with one click.       The board

      • Size and build: 108 × 46 mm, its top corners curved to follow the inside of the case (import board.dxf for the exact edge), two copper layers, 1.2 mm thick (the stick's legs are made for a 1.2 mm board), with ENIG gold finish. Gold is not optional: the face buttons press carbon onto bare board contacts, and the ordinary tin finish wears and tarnishes there.
      • Everything the factory places goes on the front. Assembly on one side is the cheap service; two sides roughly doubles the set-up cost.
      • Radio module at the bottom edge, in the middle, antenna end down, between the grips and away from both hands. The board has a 20 × 6 mm cut-out under the antenna, as Espressif's layout guide asks. Espressif also asks for 15 mm of clear space between the antenna and any housing, which a hand-held case cannot give, and the battery behind the board ends 1.6 mm short of the antenna; expect less range than the module's rated best until it is measured.
      • USB-C socket in the middle of the top edge, its mouth about 0.5 mm past the edge, with the two charge lights beside it and the slide switch to its left.
      • Stick on the left, face contacts on the right. The stick needs fourteen holes: four of 1.2 mm for the frame legs, six of 1.0 mm for the sensor pins, four of 0.9 mm for the switch. Their positions, taken from the datasheet, are in layout.json.
      • Face contacts: for each button, two interleaved combs of 0.3 mm fingers with 0.3 mm gaps, in a circle about 5.3 mm across, with the green solder mask removed over the whole circle. One comb goes to the pin, the other to ground. The spacing in the drawing below (27 mm left to right, 21 mm top to bottom) is taken from an open-hardware SNES controller board by Taylor Madeak (CC BY-SA 4.0). Check it against the pad you bought, and check whether its domes sit square or slightly tilted, before ordering boards.
      • Shoulder switches along the top edge, their bodies flush with it and their push-buttons sticking out past it. Small switches for Select, Start and Home in a diamond with the status light, and BOOT near the bottom edge. BOOT has no hole in the case: it is only for recovering from bad firmware, and you open the case to reach it.
      • Tracks: 0.5 mm or wider for anything carrying battery or USB power, 0.25 mm for signals, the back layer one solid ground area. Keep the USB data pair short and side by side.
      • Mounting: four 2.2 mm holes for M2 screws, plus a notch in the bottom edge, left of the radio module, for the battery lead to pass to the back. Keep the back of the board clear of solder joints at the three support points in the table below.
      • Test pads: 3.3 V, ground, battery +, TX, RX, labelled in the white print.

      What changed from version 3. The outline, the board and every part position are the same; the surfaces are not. Version 3 was a flat slab with rounded edges. In version 4 the front is a shallow dome, highest between the stick and the buttons, falling 2.6 mm to each side and nearly 6 mm down the grips. The back is flat in the middle, where the battery lies, and swells 7 mm into each palm. Each edge rolls over in one curve, 5.5 mm on the front and 8 mm on the back, with no flat step. The wall is 2 mm thick everywhere, so the inside follows the outside. Each face button's top is cut to the curve where it sits, which is why there are now four different ones.

      What it costs. A flat face can lie on the printer's plate; a curved one cannot. Both shells now print with the open rim on the plate and supports inside, which uses more plastic and time, and the gently sloping faces show fine contour lines, like a map's, where each layer ends. See Printing for how to make the best of that. If the finish matters more than the shape, version 3 is still here.

      Earlier versions, kept for reference. Each model writes its own shells, buttons and board files when run.

      • Version 3 (125 × 78 × 21.7 mm, flat faces, prints with no supports): STEP, model. Same board as version 4.
      • Version 2 (140 × 85 × 22.7 mm, with a 122 mm board): STEP, model.

      How the parts mount

      • Board: sits against four posts in the front shell, located by the four screws that pass through it, and is clamped by matching posts on the back shell. Three more posts on the back shell hold it up behind the stick and the face buttons so pressing them cannot bend it.
      • Stick: soldered to the board; the cap pushes onto its 4 mm D-shaped shaft after the case is closed.
      • Face buttons: caps drop into their holes from inside the front shell, the silicone pad lies over them with its pegs through the board, and the board holds the stack in place.
      • Shoulder buttons: each is a printed plunger sliding in a slot in the top edge, pressing the side-facing switch, with a small wing on each side, just behind the wall and wider than the slot, so it cannot pull out. Its outer end is cut to the curve of the shoulder, standing 2.2 mm out. No hinge and no spring: the switch returns it.
      • Battery: in a walled pocket in the back shell, held with thin foam tape. No screw posts, sharp edges or wire ends anywhere near it.
      • Light: a 2 mm hole over the status LED, filled with a drop of clear glue as a window.

      Assembly, step by step

      The factory has already soldered every small part. Per controller, you solder one part (the stick) and assemble.

      1. Look the board over. Check the USB-C socket and the module are flat and straight.
      2. Flash and test the bare board before soldering anything. Plug it in, upload the firmware, open the Serial Monitor, and press each switch with a fingertip; bridge each gold face contact with the tip of a metal screwdriver. Every one should show up.
      3. Solder the stick. Push it flat against the board, solder one frame leg, check it is still flat, then solder the other 13 joints. Iron at about 350 °C, 2 seconds a joint.
      4. Check the battery plug with a multimeter before it ever touches the board. Red must line up with the pad marked +. Sellers do not agree on which way round this plug is wired, and a reversed battery destroys the board. If it is reversed, lift the two metal tabs out of the plug housing and swap them.
      5. Plug in the battery, switch on. The light should blink slowly. Pair it with a computer and check every input in joy.cpl or a browser gamepad tester.
      6. Calibrate the stick: hold Start + Select for 3 seconds and follow the light.
      7. Charge test: plug in USB with the switch off. Red light on; it turns green when full.
      8. Set the six brass inserts into the front shell's posts with the soldering iron at about 220 °C. Press gently and let the heat do the work.
      9. Load the front shell, lying face-down: the four button caps, each in its own hole (top, right, bottom, left, as you hold the controller), the silicone pad, the four shoulder plungers, the small button caps, then lower the board onto its posts.
      10. Fit the battery into the back shell with foam tape, bring its lead up through the notch in the board's bottom edge, plug it into the socket beside the notch (the plug goes in from the left), and close the case without trapping the lead. Six screws, the two shorter ones in the grips; snug, not tight.
      11. Push on the thumb cap and run the full test once more.

      Time: the first one will take over an hour. Once practised, expect about 20 to 25 minutes of hands-on work per controller, so roughly 12 hours for 30, plus about 200 hours of unattended printing and a few minutes each of support removal.

      Tools you need: the list is under Tools and consumables, bought once. Practise on a solder practice kit first; the stick is not the part to learn on.

      If you sell them

      This is the part most hobby write-ups leave out, and it changes the answer. This is general information, not legal advice.

      • FCC. Because the radio is a pre-certified module used as its maker intended, the controller does not need its own radio certification. It still needs testing as an ordinary electronic device (FCC Part 15, Subpart B) and a Supplier's Declaration of Conformity. Labs quote roughly $1,500 to $2,500 for that; some quote up to $5,000. The case must be labelled "Contains FCC ID:" followed by the module's ID, and a person or company in the United States must be named as responsible. Without a certified module, radio certification runs about $8,000 to $25,000, which is the best argument for using one.
      • Bluetooth. The Bluetooth SIG requires every product that uses Bluetooth to be qualified under the seller's own membership before sale. Joining as an Adopter is free. The qualification fee, on the schedule in force since 1 March 2026, is $12,000 per design. Using a module that is already qualified removes the testing, not the fee, and no reduced fee for small makers could be found.
      • So the cost of selling legally is about $13,500 to $14,500 before the first sale: around $450 a unit at 30 units, or $70 a unit at 200. That is why small makers generally do not sell Bluetooth devices, and it is the honest answer for this project too.
      • Thirty units for friends is not automatically exempt. The FCC's exemption for home-built devices covers things that are not marketed and are built in quantities of five or fewer for personal use.
      • Also on the list: a lithium battery test summary (UN 38.3) from the cell's maker for shipping; California's embedded-battery recycling fee and labelling rules; and, if it is sold as a product for children 12 and under, third-party safety testing and a Children's Product Certificate.
      • Looks and names. Sony lists the PlayStation shapes (triangle, circle, cross, square) as its trademarks and has protected the shape of its controllers. On a unit you sell, use your own button markings and your own body shape, and do not describe it with another company's product names.

      Honest trade-offs against a store-bought controller

      • It will not save money. A good budget Bluetooth controller with two sticks, a d-pad, rumble and motion sensing costs $20 to $30 in a shop. This one costs $23 to $28 in parts plus your time, tools and prints. Build it to learn how a controller works and to own a design you can change.
      • "Standard gamepad" has limits on both systems. Windows lists it as a game controller with no driver, and Steam, browsers and emulators use it directly. But many Windows games only listen for Xbox-style controllers and will need Steam Input or a similar remapper in between. On a Mac it pairs and works in Steam, browsers and emulators, but games that use Apple's own controller system only recognise Xbox, PlayStation and a few other named pads. Some hobby firmware gets around this by pretending to be an Xbox controller; this one does not, because copying another company's product identity is not something to ship.
      • Latency is good, not the best. At 7.5 ms between radio slots a press waits about 4 ms on average, 7.5 ms at worst, before it is sent. That is fine for fast games. A commercial pad on its own USB dongle, or wired, is quicker, and this controller has no wired mode. On a Mac the gap is 11 to 15 ms.
      • Fewer inputs. No right stick, no d-pad, no analog triggers, no rumble, no motion. Great for platformers, retro and arcade games; wrong for shooters.
      • Feel. The face buttons use the same rubber-dome method as commercial pads, so the press itself should feel right. The printed shell and caps will not: layer lines, a slightly rough surface and looser fits than injection-moulded plastic.
      • Thicker than a handheld, and the curves cost finish. About 22 mm in the middle, against 14 mm for a Switch Lite, because of the full-size stick. Version 4 has a domed front and grips that swell into the palm, but a home printer builds those slopes from steps, so the faces show contour lines that a flat version 3, or a moulded controller, does not. The supports inside leave rough patches that nobody sees but that take time to clean up.
      • Durability is designed in but unproven. PETG shells, brass inserts, a supported USB socket and a contact-free stick are the right choices. A printed case is still weaker between its layers than a moulded one, and none of this has been through a term in a backpack yet.
      • No safety testing. A commercial controller's battery circuit has been tested and certified. This one uses standard, well-proven parts, but nobody has tested this board. Do not charge the first units unattended.

      What to do first

      1. Breadboard it (about $50). Buy one ESP32-C6-DevKitC-1 development board (it carries the same module), two sticks, one rubber pad set and a handful of push buttons. Wire them to the pins in the table, flash the firmware and get it working as a gamepad on your computer. This proves the firmware and tells you how the stick and pad feel before any money goes on boards. The shopping list has every item.
      2. Measure, and print the coupon. With calipers, measure the pad (dome spacing, pill size, peg positions) and the stick. Put those numbers into the case model, run it, and print the test coupon and one case.
      3. Draw the board and order 5, with 2 assembled (roughly $70 to $110 with shipping and duty). Build two complete controllers with printed cases. Expect to find at least one mistake; that is what this order is for.
      4. Use those two for a few weeks, in a bag, every day. Fix what breaks.
      5. Then order the 30.
